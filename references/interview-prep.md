# Interview prep

Per scheduled interview, not per application. Produces two files in the
job-hunt folder: a full prep document, and a one-page sheet the person reads
cold in the ten minutes before the call.

Both get written. The long document is how the knowledge gets built; the one-pager
is what survives contact with adrenaline.

## 1. Their thing, exactly

Learn the product, program or service the role serves, by name, in specifics.
What it is, who it is for, what it costs, what it competes with, what changed
about it recently.

This is the layer most candidates skip and every interviewer notices. Someone who
names the product's actual modules is having a different conversation than
someone who read the About page.

Where the company publishes a curriculum, a changelog, a public roadmap, docs, or
a pricing page, read it. Where the role is customer-facing, find out who the
customers are and what they complain about publicly.

## 2. The requirement list as a question bank

The posting's requirement list is the interview's question bank, in roughly that
order. Take each named skill and write what a competent answer sounds like.

Rank them by likelihood: the thing named first, the thing named twice, and the
thing named in both the posting and the recruiter's email are what most of the
hour will be about. Prepare those to L3 depth, per the term-to-defence chains in
`answer-bank.md`, and the rest to one solid line each.

## 3. Facts to have exact

The handful of technical points where being approximately right is worse than
saying you do not know. Get them precise, in the person's own words, with the
mechanism rather than the label.

Include the distinctions that sound similar and are not, because "these two get
confused, and here is the difference" is the single highest-value sentence
available in a technical interview.

## 4. The layer under the job title

Every role has work that the title does not say. A solutions role is a teaching
job. A platform role is a support job. A senior role is a mentoring job. Find the
layer, and prepare for it as seriously as for the technical part, because it is
usually the part that decides the hire.

Ask directly during prep: what does this person do on a bad Tuesday? Prepare an
answer for that.

## 5. Three stories, chosen in advance

Pick them before the call, from the behavioral bank in `answer-bank.md`, matched
to this posting. Each has a number and an outcome. One of them is a failure with
a real cost.

Picking in advance means the recovery from a surprise question is a lookup rather
than a search.

## 6. One line

The sentence that says why this person for this role, in their own voice, twenty
words or less. It gets used in the opening, and again in the close.

## 7. Gaps, named before they are found

List what the person does not have that the posting asks for. For each, one
sentence stating it plainly and one sentence on the nearest real thing they do
have.

Named early, a gap is judgment. Discovered late, it is a discrepancy. The
difference is entirely in who says it first.

No bluffing, on anything. "I have not done that; the closest I have is this" is a
strong answer, and a fabricated one falls apart on the follow-up, which is always
asked.

## 8. Questions to ask them

Five, written down, specific to this company. Questions that could only be asked
by someone who did the work in section 1.

At least one about how the work actually runs: what gets in the way, what the
last hire found surprising, how success is measured in the first ninety days.
Questions about the work land better than questions about the perks, and they
double as due diligence, which is the real reason to ask them.

## 9. The night before

- Read the one-pager twice. Do not read the long document again.
- Check the time in both timezones, the link, and the joining method.
- Have the CV and the portfolio open in tabs, and nothing else.
- Have water. Test the camera and the microphone.
- Sleep. A tired candidate who prepared beats a rehearsed candidate who did not
  sleep, every time.

## The one-pager

Distilled to a single screen, in this order:

1. The product, in one line, and the three names to say correctly.
2. The question bank, ranked, one line each.
3. Three stories, by title.
4. The one line.
5. Gaps, and the honest response to each.
6. Five questions to ask.
7. Delivery rules from `answer-bank.md`: sixty seconds, one claim, stop when it
   lands, numbers unprompted, no discounts volunteered.

Nothing else. A cheat sheet that needs scrolling is a document.

## After

Within an hour, write down what was asked, what landed, what did not, and any
question the answer bank could not cover. Add the new question to the bank.

By the fourth interview the bank covers most of what gets asked, and the prep
drops from three hours to forty minutes. That compounding is the entire reason
these files exist.
